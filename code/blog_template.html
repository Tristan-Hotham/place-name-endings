Grimsby, Lancaster, Llandudno, Pitlochry, Ballymena: every place name is a small fossil of the language that named it. I tagged {{meta.n_names|int}} town, village and townland names across Britain and Ireland by the old words inside them, and checked thousands of Irish spellings against their official Irish forms. Kil- turns out to mean wood far more often than you might think.

Bradford is the broad ford. Grimsby is a Norse farmstead. Lancaster is the Roman fort on the Lune, Llandudno the church of Saint Tudno, Ballymena the middle settlement. Most of us read these names every day without noticing that they are written in five or six languages: Old English, Old Norse, Latin, Welsh and Cornish, Pictish, and Gaelic.

Place-name scholars have studied them for well over a century, county by county and name by name. I wanted the whole picture at once. So I took every settlement in Ordnance Survey's open gazetteer of Great Britain, Northern Ireland's official towns and townlands, and the Republic of Ireland's official gazetteer of towns and townlands, and tagged each name with {{meta.n_rules|int}} spelling rules: endings such as -ton, -by and -chester, beginnings such as llan-, aber-, tre-, pit-, inver-, bally- and kil-. That is {{meta.n_settlements|int}} settlements and {{meta.n_townlands|int}} Irish townlands.

One caution before the findings. A spelling is not an etymology. My rules find letters, and the meanings I give are the usual readings; a single name needs a place-name dictionary. Treat each tag as a good first guess. In Ireland, as you will see, I could do better than guess.

Five languages, one map

{{coverage.settlements_any|pct}} of settlements and {{coverage.townlands_any|pct}} of townlands carry at least one element on my list. England is English: {{families.settlements.England.english|pct}} of its settlements have an English element and {{families.settlements.England.norse|pct1}} a Norse one. Wales is Welsh, {{families.settlements.Wales.brittonic|pct}} against {{families.settlements.Wales.english|pct}} English. Scotland is the most mixed, with Gaelic and English elements level at about {{families.settlements.Scotland.goidelic|pct}} each, and Norse names on {{scotland.norse_isles.Shetland Islands|pct}} of Shetland's settlements. In Ireland, Gaelic elements start {{families.townlands.Republic of Ireland.goidelic|pct}} of townland names in the Republic and {{families.townlands.Northern Ireland.goidelic|pct}} in Northern Ireland.

The single commonest element is the Old English tūn, a farmstead or estate that grew into a village: {{elements.ton.n|int}} settlements end in -ton, {{ton_share_england|pct}} of all those in England. Then come -ley, a woodland clearing ({{elements.ley.n|int}}), -ing ({{elements.ing.n|int}}) and -ham ({{elements.ham.n|int}}). Among Irish townlands, Bally- leads by a distance with {{ireland.bal_townlands|int}}, then Kil- ({{elements.kil.n_townlands|int}}) and, surprisingly, the English -town ({{elements.town.n_townlands|int}}).

Some elements are town words. The Old English ceaster, borrowed from Latin castra and used for Roman forts and towns, survives in {{elements.chester.n|int}} settlement names, and {{page.chester_town|pct}} of them are towns or cities (Manchester, Lancaster, Doncaster, Chester), against {{size.base_town_share|pct1}} of all settlements in Great Britain. Others are hamlet words: {{page.tre_hamlet|pct}} of tre- names in Wales and Cornwall are hamlets.

How sharp is the Welsh border?

Language borders on maps are usually drawn as lines, but are they? I measured every settlement's distance from the land border between England and Wales and applied the Welsh rules (llan-, pen-, aber-, tre-, cwm, bryn, nant and the rest) on both sides.

It is sharp. Between 20 and 40 km inside Wales, {{border.welsh_20_40_w|pct}} of settlements have a Welsh element; at the same distance inside England, {{border.welsh_20_40_e|pct1}}. But the line is not a cliff. {{border.welsh_in_england_n|int}} English settlements near the border have Welsh spellings, from Llanyblodwel and Llanfair Waterdine in Shropshire to Pontrilas and Llangrove in Herefordshire, and English names reach into Wales the other way.

Tre, Pol and Pen

"By Tre, Pol and Pen shall ye know the Cornishmen", goes the rhyme, and the data agree. With the Cornish rules run in both counties, {{cornwall.cornwall_share|pct}} of Cornwall's settlements carry a Cornish element and {{cornwall.devon_share|pct1}} of Devon's, most of those false friends such as Rosemary Lane. There are {{cornwall.tre_cornwall|int}} tre- settlements in Cornwall and {{cornwall.tre_devon|int}} in Devon. The share barely falls across Cornwall, then stops at the Tamar.

Aber or inver?

Scotland has two words for a river mouth. Aber- is Brittonic, from the Britons and the Picts; inver- is Gaelic. On my count there are {{scotland.aber|int}} aber- settlements in Scotland and {{scotland.inver|int}} inver- ones, and {{scotland.aber_east_of_4w|int}} of the abers lie east of 4 degrees west, in Perth and Kinross, Fife and Aberdeenshire. The Picts' pit-, a share of land, survives in {{scotland.pit|int}} settlement names, all but a few in the east: Pitlochry, Pittenweem, Pitmedden.

Ireland: when Kil- means wood

Ireland's names live in its townlands, about {{meta.n_townlands|int}} small land units. In the Republic, the official gazetteer gives an Irish form for {{irish_forms.n_townlands_irish|int}} of them, which lets me check what an English spelling actually stands for. Most beginnings are what they seem: Knock- is cnoc, a hill, {{irish_forms.rows.knock.agree_share|pct}} of the time, Drum- is droim, a ridge, {{irish_forms.rows.drum.agree_share|pct}}, and Bally- is baile, a settlement, {{irish_forms.rows.bal.agree_share|pct}}. Even there, {{irish_forms.bal.beal|int}} Bally- names are Béal, the mouth of a ford, as in Ballina, Béal an Átha.

Kil- is the big exception. It is usually explained as cill, a church or monastic cell, and many Kil- names are just that. But of {{irish_forms.kil.n|int}} Kil- names with an official Irish form, {{irish_forms.kil.coill|int}} ({{page.kil_coill_share|pct}}) are coill, a wood. And the wood names cluster: in Monaghan, {{irish_forms.kil_townlands.counties.0.1|int}} of {{irish_forms.kil_townlands.counties.0.2|int}} Kil- townlands are coill; in Kilkenny, {{irish_forms.kil_townlands.counties.-1.1|int}} of {{irish_forms.kil_townlands.counties.-1.2|int}}.

Bally- has its own geography. In Ards and North Down {{page.ards_bal|pct}} of townlands start with Bally-, in Wexford {{page.wex_bal|pct}}, but in Leitrim, Monaghan, Cavan and Fermanagh and Omagh under 2% do; their townlands are Drums, Kils, Lisses and Derrys. And in Meath {{elements.town.top_share_areas.0.3|pct}} of townlands end in the English -town, as do {{elements.town.top_share_areas.1.3|pct}} in Dublin and {{elements.town.top_share_areas.2.3|pct}} in Kildare: the map still shows the English-speaking Pale of the Middle Ages.

How I did this

The names come from OS Open Names (all {{meta.gb_n|int}} populated places in Great Britain), OSNI Open Data ({{meta.ni_places_n|int}} towns and villages and {{meta.ni_townlands_n|int}} townlands in Northern Ireland) and Tailte Éireann's National Placenames Gazetteer ({{meta.roi_cop_n|int}} towns and villages and {{meta.roi_townlands_n|int}} townlands in the Republic, with official Irish forms). Each name is split into words, qualifiers such as Upper and Little are dropped, and each word is tested against the rules. Some rules only run where the language was spoken, so Balham and Kilburn in London are not counted as Gaelic. A random sample of {{audit.n|int}} matches was read through for obvious false friends during the analysis; {{audit.false|int}} turned up, such as Tucking Mill caught by -ing.

The limits matter. The datasets differ (Britain's goes down to hamlets, the Irish town lists only to larger places, which is why the townlands are there), modern spellings can hide or fake an element, and nothing here dates a name. On the map you can look up any town, village or townland and see which elements its name carries and what they usually mean. My earlier study Beck, brook or burn? covers the Norse line and the Danelaw in more detail.
